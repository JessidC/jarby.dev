01

Read the data before trusting any model.

10,000+client companies, in Colombia and other countries

What was at stake. SIESA is one of the largest ERP vendors in Colombia for large companies. Its software runs their payroll, finance and operations, so when a client gets stuck, the support chat decides whether that company's day moves or stops.

Support quality weighs on every renewal. I own the platform that answers those chats, so making that service better was my job.

That chat ran on a third-party chatbot whose answers we couldn't measure or control. I started by pulling its production conversations and reading what clients actually went through.

90 daysof production data
55,000+chats analyzed
6decisions taken to leadership

What the data showed

  1. A black box. There was no way to know whether an answer was correct. Quality could not be managed, only guessed.

  2. No control over sources. The model and the sources sat with the vendor, so a rule against invented answers could not be enforced from outside.

  3. The handoff mattered as much as the bot. When customers asked for a person, the handoff to agents needed a redesign of its own.

DecisionBuild an engine the company owns and can measure, and redesign the handoff to people in the same move.

I took it to leadership as a plan built on those 90 days, with six decisions ranked by impact and cost.

02

Design an engine where every layer can be measured.

Seven design decisions. Each one answers a problem the data exposed: no visibility, no control, a handoff that needed work.

01

No framework in the request path
Why

I needed full control over latency, token cost and traceability. Every abstraction between me and the model is something I can't measure or debug.

In practice

Python and FastAPI straight on the model API. Frameworks are great for orchestration around the engine, and Jarvis uses LangGraph, but the answer path had to be audited line by line.

02

Claude on AWS Bedrock, running on EC2
Why

SIESA already ran on AWS. Billing, permissions and secrets stayed inside the account the company already had, with no new vendor to bring in.

In practice

Claude Sonnet answers the customer. Claude Haiku rewrites follow-up questions, writes context at ingestion and reads scanned pages. A Sonnet versus Haiku benchmark on quality, latency, cost and escalation backs the split.

03

Behind the old bot's exact contract
Why

The support portal didn't have to change. Customers kept the same chat window; only the engine behind it changed.

In practice

The service exposes the same interface the old chatbot had, so each module can switch to the new engine, or back, with one setting.

04

Dense retrieval, then a reranker
Why

Retrieval is cheap and wide; a cross-encoder is expensive and precise. Retrieve broadly, then let Cohere Rerank choose what the model actually reads.

In practice

pgvector with an HNSW index, top 20 filtered by ERP module, reranked to the top 5 with reserved slots for curated answers and process templates. Chapter 4 covers the alternative I built and retired.

05

Validate every answer before it ships
Why

This is the direct answer to the black box. Nothing reaches the customer until it passes the checks.

In practice

Cited sources must exist in what was retrieved. A number that isn't in the retrieved passages sends the chat to a person. Banned phrases are blocked, and modules that need it get an extra grounding check.

06

Hand over honestly instead of inventing
Why

A confident wrong answer is worse than a clean handoff. When the evidence is weak, the engine says so.

In practice

About 170 deterministic rules decide before the model: asks for a person, closing, risk, out of scope. Below a confidence floor the engine rescues from the last useful turn, uses a process template or offers an advisor, and the model can hand over with a reason.

07

Shadow mode before the switch
Why

No customer should be the test. The new engine answered real traffic in parallel, without being shown, and an independent judge compared both engines.

In practice

772 real conversations before launch: the judge preferred the new engine 44% of the time, against 40% for the old one.

03

Ship it one module at a time.

This is the path a customer question takes. Every step leaves a trace with latency, tokens and cost.

One request, end to end
  1. RulesDeterministic intent rules handle greetings, closings, risk and requests for a person before any model call.
  2. RewriteHaiku turns a follow-up like "it still fails" into a standalone question.
  3. Curated answerA close match with an expert-approved answer is returned as written, with no generation.
  4. Retrievepgvector search, filtered by ERP module.
  5. RerankCohere Rerank keeps the top 5. Below a confidence floor, the engine rescues or hands over instead of guessing.
  6. GenerateSonnet answers from the sources, with prompt caching and a tool to hand over to a person.
  7. ValidateCitations, numbers and grounding are checked against what was retrieved.

04

The lever was the content, not the method.

Hybrid retrieval sounds like the obvious upgrade. I built it, measured it twice, and turned it off. The data pointed somewhere else.

The method

Hybrid BM25 + vectors, fused with RRF

With real customer queries it recovered 0 of 5 failing cases: in Spanish, a keyword query behaves like an AND and empties the candidate pool.

3.6 points

Every retrieval variant fit inside that margin. On the English Odoo docs in rag-eval-bench the same technique won by 12.5 points. Same idea, opposite result, which is why every change gets measured on its own data.

The content

Where the effort went instead

  • 1,027 documents reprocessed with multimodal OCR, isolating the fifth of the index an earlier OCR had damaged.

  • An improvement loop that turns the agents' answers into new knowledge every week. Chapter 6 tells how it works.

  • A curation console with roles and per-module permissions, used by 44 agent accounts.

  • Fixes found by replay. Correcting one index search parameter raised the mean rerank score by 0.054 and made 15% of the low-confidence handoffs answerable, without a single case getting worse.

05

Measure every release like a product.

Nothing goes to production without a baseline, a replay and a way back. Payroll went first, so it has the longest series and the one I tested for significance. Financial, Commercial and Zeus, the other critical lines, have moved the same way.

Cost per turn after adding prompt caching. Every model call is metered.

How it's measured

Each module has a golden set of 30 cases scored by an LLM judge, and the score gates the release. Every change is replayed A/B on real traces first, and before and after comparisons get significance tests.

Two judges, on purpose: a blind judge from a different model family, so the generator never grades its own family, and a judge anchored on the retrieved evidence. A simpler version of this method is public as rag-eval-bench. Its card below says what differs from production.

06

Let the agents teach the engine.

When the engine hands a chat to a person, the agent's answer is the best evidence of what it was missing. A separate pipeline turns those answers into new knowledge, and an expert decides what goes in.

From a handoff to new knowledge
  1. An agent closes the chatResolutions reach the engine through a data bridge from the support portal every 30 minutes.
  2. Only real gaps continueHandoffs caused by staffing, frustration or risk are dropped before any model call.
  3. A judge reads the agent's turnsFrom a different model family than the one that answers. It rebuilds the question and the reusable answer with its evidence, and rejects what can't be documented, like "open a ticket".
  4. Compared with what the engine knowsAlready covered is skipped. A repeat adds demand to the pending item. An answer that contradicts indexed content is flagged as outdated.
  5. An expert approves or edits itFrom the curation console. Approved answers are served right away, with no reindex.

What it never does

  • Publish anything an expert hasn't approved
  • Let the answering model grade its own work
  • Pay to judge the same content twice, thanks to a content fingerprint
  • Learn from handoffs that were a staffing problem
About 30 to 60 minutes from a closed chat to a candidate in the queue.
50%40% human escalation in Commercial, in round numbers

Commercial's drop came mostly from this loop. It adds about 150 approved answers a week for roughly US$10 a month in model cost, and 44 agent accounts curate from the console with roles and permissions per module.

It is the main reason the engine keeps improving between releases. I rebuilt it in the open as support-learning-loop.

07

Measure the whole service, beyond the bot.

An engine is one piece of a service. With the RAG live, I went after what happens around every conversation.

30%64% customer-service CSAT

Redesigning the handoff

A cap on concurrent chats per agent, true first-in, first-out by arrival time, and load balancing across teams. CSAT went from 30% to 64%, measured with the weekly survey launched with the pilot.

Primary routing to agents cut first response from 20.2 to 15.2 minutes and peak load per agent from 27 to 20 chats.

Jarvis

The judge of the support service, in production. Every day it reads each conversation and decides who resolved it, the AI, an agent or no one, with the same rule for both. At closing it types the ticket, replacing a classifier whose attribution didn't hold up against a ticket-by-ticket audit.

$0.002

What it costs to judge one conversation. It reads all of them, every day.

It grew out of the conversation judge I already ran on the RAG's data. Same engine, now orchestrated in LangGraph with typed state, parallel judges, PostgreSQL checkpoints and pauses for human review. Results go to Power BI and to a daily report the support team reads in Teams.

rag-eval-bench

A bench that decides whether a RAG change ships: a golden set with questions the docs can't answer, an LLM judge, a validator that catches invented citations and numbers, and a paired McNemar test behind a CI gate. It runs on the public Odoo 18 docs.

What it found: hybrid retrieval beat dense by 12.5 points, p = 0.02. A reranker added 1.4 points and pushed p95 latency from 2.5 s to 8.3 s, so it doesn't ship.

Unlike production, one model answers and judges here, and the golden set was drafted by a model and not yet reviewed by a person. The README lists both limits.
